The lamplighter of Hollowmere began his rounds an hour before dusk, as his father had, and his father's mother before that. He carried a long brass pole with a hook at one end and a wick at the other, and a satchel of oil that sloshed against his hip with every step.

Nobody in the village remembered why the lamps had to be lit so early. The sun was still well above the hills when he reached the first post by the mill, and the light he coaxed from the glass was pale and useless against the afternoon. Children laughed at him. Old men shook their heads. He lit the lamps anyway, one after another, down the lane and over the bridge and up the long slope toward the church.

There were forty-one lamps in Hollowmere. He had counted them every day for twenty years, and every day there had been forty-one. On the evening the stranger arrived, there were forty-two.

The new lamp stood at the edge of the old orchard, where the road gave out and the trees grew thick and black. Its iron was not rusted like the others. Its glass was clean. And when he raised his pole to light it, he found that it was already burning, with a flame so cold and blue that his breath fogged in front of it.

He lowered the pole slowly. Beneath the lamp, half hidden in the long grass, someone had left a pair of boots, neatly placed side by side, the laces tied together in a careful knot. They were his size. They were, he realized with a feeling like falling, his own boots, the ones he had buried with his father eleven winters ago.

Behind him, down in the village, the other forty-one lamps went out all at once.

He did not turn around. He had been taught, as his father had been taught, that the lamps were never for the village. They were for whatever waited past the orchard, to show it where the living ended. And now there was a new light, and it was showing the way in.

Somewhere among the trees, a voice he had not heard in eleven years called his name, gently, the way it used to when supper was ready.

The lamplighter set down his satchel, untied the knot in the laces, and began to put on the boots.
